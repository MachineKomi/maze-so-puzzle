# Five couch co-op pitches for Maze so Puzzle

> **Exploratory pitches — not approved product canon.** These ideas preserve the existing solo adventure as the default, complete game. The fifth pitch adds a clearly separate set of two-player-only mazes; it would never gate solo progress, story completion, rescues, or rewards.

## 1. Miri's Wishwing Courier — the best all-round co-op

Player 2 becomes Miri, a tiny magical wish-sprite who flits around Ame's current view, sails over scenery, and turns every screen into a little shared playground. Ame still walks the maze, chooses the route, collects every real key and tool, faces the friendly guardians, and finishes the adventure. Miri is not a shortcut through the puzzle—she is Ame's energetic partner inside it.

Miri spots Duo-only Wish Motes, catches one, and flies it back to Ame for a sparkling handoff that fills a small Friendship Focus meter. She can spend that Focus to place a friendly “Look here!” marker, wake a funny environmental reaction, or cast **Dazzle & Compare** on a visible guardian so both players see the exact Power contest together. The numbers never change, but the moment becomes a shared plan: “We need to come back,” followed later by “Now we're ready!”

**Why it would be fun:** Miri gets movement skill, target choices, resource timing, comedy, and visible credit, while Ame remains the hero who actually solves the maze. The handoffs and guardian celebrations create frequent warm-fuzzy “we did that together” beats. **Trade-offs:** Mote density must be tuned carefully or Miri will feel busy but unimportant; Ame's camera can sometimes interrupt Miri's plan; and the strict ban on real-item collection is essential even when fetching a key looks tempting. This is my strongest balance of fun, identity, safety, and feasibility.

## 2. Sprig's Mischief Garden — the happiest, funniest version

Player 2 controls a magical projection of Sprig, turning the maze into a secret garden of jokes, surprises, and tiny acts of friendship. While Ame solves the real route, Sprig can tickle sleepy flowers, ring mushroom bells, chase cheeky lights, wake background creatures, and discover optional Duo curiosities hidden in places both players can already see. The fantasy is not “assistant”; it is “mischievous friend who makes the whole world feel alive.”

The best discoveries ask both players to participate. Sprig might uncover a Friendship Bloom, but Ame must choose to walk near it before Sprig can wake it; Ame might rescue a friend, then Sprig gets the final celebratory flourish; one player finds the opportunity and the other completes the moment. None of these interactions opens a real door, grants Power, removes a hazard, or changes the solution. They create an optional layer of duet secrets over every existing maze.

**Why it would be fun:** this is the most immediately charming pitch for children, gives Player 2 lots to touch, and creates laughter without turning the parent into a back-seat solver. It also gives the art, animation, VFX, and audio a wonderful co-op showcase. **Trade-offs:** it needs more bespoke content than it first appears, Sprig's story availability may not fit every campaign chapter, and the activities could feel disconnected from the puzzle if too many are merely decorative. The duet discoveries must feel authored and surprising, not like a checklist of sparkles.

## 3. Poggle's Pocket Maplight — the clever guide-and-hero partnership

Player 2 becomes Poggle's living Maplight: part flying companion, part puzzle detective. Ame explores and acts; the Maplight remembers what the pair has seen, studies the known map, compares Ame's Bag and Power with visible obstacles, and helps the players agree on a plan. This is the pitch for a parent who loves guiding without taking the controller—or for a child who delights in being the one who notices the answer first.

The Maplight can fly around the current view, place one simple “Look!” marker, circle a visible landmark, and build a tiny three-symbol plan ribbon such as **Boots → blue door → treasure** using only information Ame has already revealed. It never uncovers fog, calculates a hidden route, moves Ame, or declares the solution automatically. Player 2 still has a tactile toy to control, but their real game is memory, observation, and communication: “Wait, wasn't there a red door near the moon pond?”

**Why it would be fun:** it turns the conversation itself into the co-op mechanic and works beautifully for mixed motor skill, reading ability, or gaming experience. A thoughtful Player 2 can be genuinely valuable without altering a single maze rule. **Trade-offs:** this is the easiest role to turn into bossy back-seat driving, and a child may find it less glamorous than controlling Ame. Markers need strict limits, clues must never reveal information for free, and the flying/emote layer must remain playful enough that the Maplight feels like a character rather than a second mouse cursor.

## 4. Pass the Star — both players get to be the hero

Instead of pretending one role can satisfy everyone forever, **Pass the Star** makes role-swapping part of the fantasy. One player controls Ame while the other controls a cheerful Co-Star who flies, marks visible discoveries, catches harmless Duo glimmers, and celebrates Ame's victories. At the home screen, between mazes, or at a safe Friendship Star, either player can invite a swap: the Co-Star showers Ame in light, the controllers exchange roles, and the adventure continues without resetting the maze.

The rhythm becomes wonderfully social. A parent can guide during a hard planning section and then hand Ame back for the triumphant route; siblings can trade after each guardian or rescue; a child who first chooses the companion can decide, with no embarrassment, that they want a turn as Ame. The companion role stays deliberately simple because nobody is trapped in it, while the swap itself becomes a moment of trust: “You found the way—now you lead us there.”

**Why it would be fun:** it is unusually strong for mixed-skill families, gives both people ownership of the adventure, and requires almost no change to maze logic. It also provides a graceful answer if Amelia loves both roles. **Trade-offs:** this is more turn-sharing than deep simultaneous co-op, frequent swaps could interrupt the pace, and an adult might still dominate by holding Ame too long. The invitation must require both players' agreement, and playtesting must show that the Co-Star remains enjoyable even when nobody wants to swap immediately.

## 5. Twin Trails — a separate book of true two-player mazes

This is the big, exciting swing: keep the entire solo campaign untouched, then add a separate **Twin Trails** book made expressly for two people. Ame travels the ground maze while Player 2 follows a magical sky-path of stars, perches, and floating threads. Neither character can simply do the other's job. Ame might open the view that reveals a sky branch; the companion might carry light to an optional star switch that opens Ame's next Duo gate; both might have to reach matching friendship symbols to free a lost creature together.

These levels can embrace cooperation that the normal campaign must avoid: simultaneous choices, call-and-response clues, safe timing puzzles, complementary routes, and moments where each player sees only half of a pattern. The companion still never steals Ame's keys or Power, but here their own route is genuinely consequential. A generous reset, unmistakable symbols, no lives or blame-heavy failure, and frequent reunions would keep the mood warm rather than stressful. Finishing a maze should feel like solving a secret handshake together.

**Why it would be fun:** Twin Trails has the highest ceiling for real communication, equal role pride, replayable teamwork, and unforgettable “we couldn't have done that alone” moments. It also lets the designers protect every solo maze instead of squeezing deep co-op into rules that were never built for it. **Trade-offs:** it is effectively a second game mode with new levels, two-character rules, dedicated testing, controller requirements, saves, hints, and a co-op solver or certification process. It cannot be the first experiment—but if Amelia loves the safer companion prototype and asks for more, this is the version worth dreaming toward.

## Creative-director recommendation

Pitch all five to Amelia without calling one the “easy” role. My starting bet is **Miri's Wishwing Courier** because it offers the strongest everyday compromise: both players are active, cooperation is visible, and the solo puzzle remains intact. If Amelia responds most strongly to comedy and characters, fold in the best parts of **Sprig's Mischief Garden**; if she wants moments that truly require both players, treat **Twin Trails** as a separate future expansion rather than weakening the solo campaign to make room for it.
